1. The Gambler's Dilemma

Imagine a high-stakes casino host offers you a choice between two dice games. You can only play one, and your goal is to win cash. Before making your choice, let's break down how both games work:

🎲 Game A: Single Die (4 Rolls)

You roll one 6-sided die up to 4 times. You win immediately if a 6 appears on roll 1, roll 2, roll 3, or roll 4. You only lose if you roll no 6s in all 4 tries (e.g., landing 2, 4, 1, 5).

🎲🎲 Game B: Two Dice (24 Rolls)

You roll a pair of 6-sided dice up to 24 times. You win if you get a double-6 (6 and 6 simultaneously) on any of your 24 turns. You lose if you complete 24 rolls without ever seeing a double-6.

Why 24 rolls? Notice that getting a single 6 has a 1 in 6 chance $(1/6)$, and 4 rolls gives $4 \times (1/6) = 2/3$ proportional odds. Meanwhile, a double-6 has a 1 in 36 chance $(1/36)$, and 24 rolls gives $24 \times (1/36) = 2/3$ proportional odds! They seem mathematically equivalent... right?

Now trust your intuition: Which option would you choose?

2. Historical Context

In 1654, a French nobleman and passionate gambler named Chevalier de Méré faced this exact decision! He was losing a fortune on dice bets and wrote in desperation to mathematician Blaise Pascal. Pascal and Pierre de Fermat realized that to solve de Méré's puzzle, one must establish precise mathematical definitions for probability.

📐 Key Probability Definitions

  • Experiment: A repeatable procedure that has a well-defined set of possible results.
  • Outcome: A result of an experiment.
  • Sample Space ($S$): The set of all possible outcomes of an experiment. It is usually denoted by $S$.
  • Sample Point: An element of the sample space.
  • Event: A subset of the sample space.

3. Types of Events in Sample Space $S$

Once the sample space $S$ is established, we classify different types of subsets (events) based on their structure:

🏷️ Event Classification

  • Simple Event: Consists of exactly one sample point — for example, the event of rolling a 4 on a die is represented by $\{4\}$.
  • Compound Event: Includes two or more sample points, such as the event "rolling an even number," which corresponds to the set $\{2, 4, 6\}$.
  • Impossible Event: An event that cannot occur at all, represented by the empty set $\emptyset$.
  • Sure Event: An event that is guaranteed to happen, which means it is equal to the sample space itself ($S$).

5. Combining Events

Real-world algorithms combine simple events using set operations:

  • Intersection ($A \cap B$): BOTH $A$ AND $B$ occur simultaneously.
  • Complement ($A^c$): Event $A$ does NOT occur.

7. Kolmogorov's 3 Rules of Probability (1933)

In 1933, Russian mathematician Andrey Kolmogorov unified probability into 3 indestructible axioms:

$$1. \ P(A) \ge 0 \quad \text{(Non-negativity)}$$ $$2. \ P(S) = 1 \quad \text{(Total Certainty of Universe / Sure Event)}$$ $$3. \ \text{If } A \cap B = \emptyset, \text{ then } P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)$$

8. Probability Boundaries

Combining Kolmogorov's Axioms gives us one of the most fundamental theorems in probability theory: every event $E$ must exist within fixed boundary limits.

Probability Boundaries Theorem: Since $E \subseteq S$, the probability of any event $E$ is always bounded: $$0 \le P(E) \le 1$$
  • $P(E) = 0$: The event is an impossible event ($\emptyset$).
  • $P(E) = 1$: The event is a sure event ($S$).

9. Resolving Chevalier de Méré's Paradox

Now we have all the tools—Sample Spaces, Complement Events ($E^c$), and Axioms—to solve Chevalier de Méré's 1654 gambling paradox!

GAME B SIMULATOR: 24 Rolls of Two Dice

?
Games Played: 0 | Wins (≥1 double-6): 0 (0%)

For two 6-sided dice, the total sample space $S$ contains $6 \times 6 = 36$ outcomes. The probability of NOT landing a double-six on a single turn is $\frac{35}{36}$. Over 24 independent rolls, the chance of failing every time is:

$$P(\text{No double 6 in 24 rolls}) = \left(\frac{35}{36}\right)^{24} \approx 0.5086$$

Using the Complement Rule ($P(E) = 1 - P(E^c)$), the actual win rate for Game B is:

$$P(\text{At least one double 6}) = 1 - 0.5086 = \mathbf{0.4914} \quad (\mathbf{49.14\%})$$

Because $49.14\% < 50\%$, Chevalier de Méré was playing a losing game over the long run! Pascal and Fermat's analysis laid the foundational bedrock of modern Probability Theory.
