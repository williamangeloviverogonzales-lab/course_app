1. Why Speed Alone Can Cause Collisions

Imagine an automated forklift moving across a busy factory floor at $15 ext{ km/h}$. Knowing its speed tells you how fast it travels, but not where it is heading. To prevent collisions with workers or machinery, operators must know both its speed and direction (its velocity).

In industrial automation, structural design, and robotics, physical quantities that combine magnitude with direction are known as Vectors.

2. Scalars vs. Vectors

Physical quantities are fundamentally divided into two categories based on whether they involve direction.

  • Scalar Quantity: Described by magnitude (size) and unit only. No direction!
    Examples: Mass ($5\text{ kg}$), Temperature ($25^\circ\text{C}$), Time ($10\text{ s}$), Speed ($60\text{ km/h}$), Volume ($2\text{ L}$).
  • Vector Quantity: Described by magnitude AND direction.
    Examples: Displacement ($5t\text{ m}$ East), Velocity ($60\text{ km/h}$ North), Force ($100\text{ N}$ downward), Acceleration ($9.8\text{ m/s}^2$ downward), Momentum.

3. Graphical Vector Representation & Scale

Graphically, a vector is drawn as an arrow:

  • Length of Arrow: Represents magnitude (using a chosen graphical scale).
  • Arrowhead: Indicates direction.
  • Tail: The starting point (origin).
Understanding Scale: If a force of $50\text{ N}$ to the right is drawn as a $5\text{ cm}$ arrow, the scale is $1\text{ cm} = 10\text{ N}$.
Following this scale, a $100\text{ N}$ force would be drawn as a $10\text{ cm}$ arrow.

4. Unit Vectors & Algebraic Notation

Vectors can also be written algebraically using unit vectors $\hat{i}$ (along x-axis) and $\hat{j}$ (along y-axis).

$$\vec{F} = F_x \hat{i} + F_y \hat{j}$$ Where $F_x$ is the horizontal component and $F_y$ is the vertical component.

Example: $\vec{F} = 40\hat{i} + 30\hat{j}\text{ N}$ means $40\text{ N}$ horizontally to the right and $30\text{ N}$ vertically upward.
What is the horizontal component ($F_x$) of a force vector $ ec{F} = -15\hat{i} + 25\hat{j} ext{ N}$?

5. The Resultant Vector ($ ec{R}$)

In real industrial systems (like trusses, crane cables, or robotic arms), multiple forces act simultaneously. Calculating their combined effect is critical.

Definition 5.1: Resultant Vector ($ ec{R}$)
The single, equivalent vector that produces the exact same physical effect as all individual vectors combined together.
If two tugboats pull a heavy barge with forces $ ec{F}_1$ and $ ec{F}_2$, what does the resultant vector $ ec{R}$ represent?

6. Graphical Addition: Parallelogram Method

Used when two vectors start from the same common point (e.g., two workers pulling a machine component).

Steps:
  1. Draw vectors $ ec{A}$ and $ ec{B}$ starting from a common origin.
  2. Complete the parallelogram by drawing parallel dashed lines from their tips.
  3. Draw resultant $ ec{R}$ as the diagonal from the origin to the opposite corner.

7. Graphical Addition: Polygon (Head-to-Tail) Method

Used when adding two or more vectors sequentially (e.g., a robotic arm making consecutive movements).

Steps:
  1. Draw first vector $ ec{A}$ starting from the origin.
  2. Draw tail of second vector $ ec{B}$ attached directly to the head (tip) of $ ec{A}$.
  3. Draw third vector $ ec{C}$ attached to head of $ ec{B}$.
  4. Draw resultant $ ec{R}$ from the tail of the first vector to the head of the last vector.
A robotic arm moves $4 ext{ m}$ East, then $3 ext{ m}$ North. Using the Head-to-Tail method, what is its resultant displacement? ($\sqrt{4^2 + 3^2}$)

8. Graphical Vector Subtraction

Subtracting a vector is equivalent to adding its negative (reversing its direction by $180^\circ$).

$$ ec{A} - ec{B} = ec{A} + (- ec{B})$$ Application: Change in Velocity ($\Delta ec{v}$)
In machinery dynamics, change in velocity is defined as $\Delta ec{v} = ec{v}_f - ec{v}_i$.
If vector $ ec{B}$ points due East ($0^\circ$), in which direction does $- ec{B}$ point?

9. Analytical Method: Resolving Components

While graphical methods give visual insight, the Analytical Component Method provides high mathematical precision required for engineering.

For a vector $ ec{V}$ with magnitude $V$ at angle $ heta$ with the positive x-axis: $$V_x = V \cos( heta)$$ $$V_y = V \sin( heta)$$
Resolve a force $F = 100 ext{ N}$ acting at $ heta = 30^\circ$ above the positive x-axis. What is its horizontal component $F_x$? ($100 \cos 30^\circ$)

10. Recombining Components into Resultant & Quadrants

After summing all horizontal components ($R_x = \sum V_x$) and vertical components ($R_y = \sum V_y$), recombine them using Pythagorean theorem and trigonometry:

$$R = \sqrt{R_x^2 + R_y^2}$$ $$ heta = rctan\left(\left| rac{R_y}{R_x} ight| ight)$$
Quadrant Rule:
  • Quadrant 1 ($R_x > 0, R_y > 0$): $ heta_{std} = heta$
  • Quadrant 2 ($R_x < 0, R_y > 0$): $ heta_{std} = 180^\circ - heta$
  • Quadrant 3 ($R_x < 0, R_y < 0$): $ heta_{std} = 180^\circ + heta$
  • Quadrant 4 ($R_x > 0, R_y < 0$): $ heta_{std} = 360^\circ - heta$

2D Vector Resultant Calculator 
import math

# Vector inputs: (magnitude, angle_in_degrees)
forces = [
    (100, 30),
    (150, 120)
]

rx = 0.0
ry = 0.0

for mag, deg in forces:
    rad = math.radians(deg)
    rx += mag * math.cos(rad)
    ry += mag * math.sin(rad)

r_mag = math.sqrt(rx**2 + ry**2)
r_angle = math.degrees(math.atan2(ry, rx))

print(f"Resultant Rx: {rx:.2f} N, Ry: {ry:.2f} N")
print(f"Resultant Force: {r_mag:.2f} N at {r_angle:.2f}°")
